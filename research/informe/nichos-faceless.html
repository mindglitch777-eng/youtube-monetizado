What Did Ancient Humans Do All Day?

All research and sources are linked below.

📚 RESEARCH & SOURCES:

▸ Richard Lee's work-hours study — "What Hunters Do for a Living, or, How to Make Out on Scarce Resources" (1968, in Man the Hunter, Aldine Publishing). Lee's fieldwork with the Ju/'hoansi beginning in 1963 recorded roughly 17 hours a week spent acquiring food.

▸ The Original Affluent Society — "Stone Age Economics" by Marshall Sahlins (1972, Aldine-Atherton). The essay that reframed hunter-gatherers as the first affluent society.

▸ Skeletal health decline with farming — "Paleopathology at the Origins of Agriculture" edited by Mark Nathan Cohen & George Armelagos (1984, Academic Press). Shorter stature, more cavities, more joint disease among early farmers compared to the hunter-gatherers before them.

▸ Endurance running & persistence hunting — "Endurance running and the evolution of Homo" (2004, Nature) https://doi.org/10.1038/nature03052 — sweating and heat dissipation let humans outlast faster prey.

▸ Blombos Cave shell beads, South Africa — "Middle Stone Age Shell Beads from South Africa" (2004, Science) https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1095905 — perforated sea snail shells worn as ornaments over 70,000 years ago.

▸ Hohle Fels flute, Germany — "New flutes document the earliest musical tradition in southwestern Germany" (2009, Nature) https://doi.org/10.1038/nature08169 — a bird-bone flute with five finger holes, roughly 40,000 years old, excavated in 2008.

▸ Polly Wiessner — "Embers of society: Firelight talk among the Ju/'hoansi Bushmen" (2014, Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences) https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1404212111 — 81% of firelight conversation turned to stories.

▸ Social time among the Ju/'hoansi — "Affluence Without Abundance: The Disappearing World of the Bushmen" by James Suzman (2017, Bloomsbury). Roughly six hours a day spent on pure social time.

▸ Biphasic sleep — "Sleep We Have Lost: Pre-industrial Slumber in the British Isles" by A. Roger Ekirch (2001, The American Historical Review). Historical evidence of first and second sleep with a calm waking hour between.

▸ The Hadza — "The Hadza: Hunter-Gatherers of Tanzania" by Frank Marlowe (2010, University of California Press). Work-hour patterns matching the 15–20 hour range.

▸ The farming transition — "Guns, Germs, and Steel" by Jared Diamond (1997, W. W. Norton) and the Fertile Crescent origins of agriculture around 10,000 years ago.

————————————————————

PEOPLE & SITES MENTIONED IN THIS VIDEO:

▸ Richard Lee — Anthropologist who walked into the Kalahari in 1963 with a notebook and a stopwatch and recorded how the Ju/'hoansi actually spent every hour. His count — about 17 hours a week getting food — overturned the textbook story of constant struggle.

▸ Polly Wiessner — Anthropologist at the University of Utah. Her 2014 study compared daytime and nighttime conversations among the Ju/'hoansi Bushmen and found that over 80% of fireside talk shifted to stories, myths, and ceremonies — the hours where culture itself may have been born.

▸ James Suzman — Anthropologist who spent decades with the Ju/'hoansi and documented around six hours of pure social time every day — because in a world with no money, police, or courts, relationships are the bank account, the hospital, and the pension all at once.

▸ The Ju/'hoansi — One of the last groups on Earth still living by hunting and gathering when researchers arrived, in one of the harshest environments a human can live in — the Kalahari Desert on the edge of Botswana.

▸ Blombos Cave — Archaeological site on the southern coast of South Africa. Shell beads over 70,000 years old, drilled and strung to be worn — someone walked to the coast, collected shells, and sat for hours making something purely beautiful.

▸ Hohle Fels Cave — A cave in southern Germany where a 40,000-year-old flute carved from a vulture's wing bone was excavated in 2008 — five finger holes spaced for actual musical notes. Someone in the Ice Age had the free time to learn an instrument.

▸ The Fertile Crescent — The region in the Middle East where humans first planted grain and kept animals around 10,000 years ago — the moment the trap opened, and the moment human time started disappearing.
